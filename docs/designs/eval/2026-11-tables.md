# PDF readers, measured (2026-11-27)

kb-author's own PDF reader (unpdf, with its table readers in `packages/kb-author/src/extract/tables/`) was measured
against [LiteParse](https://github.com/run-llama/liteparse) (`@llamaindex/liteparse` 2.21.3, Apache-2.0: PDFium and a
layout pass in Rust, with Node bindings prebuilt for each platform), in two trials, then on kb-author's own scorer.

## The first trial: six documents ours was tuned on

Six US health plans' summaries of benefits and coverage (one template every plan fills), 7 to 11 pages each.

- **Tables.** Ours kept 9 of 9 sampled rows of the main benefits grid together; LiteParse's Markdown ran rows into
  each other (none of 9 clean), and its learned table model (OCRia-nano's) 8 of 9.
- **Headings.** Ours cut each document into 16 to 31 sections at its real headings; LiteParse's own headings were
  poor (none found by its blocks on four of the six).
- **A scanned copy** of two pages: ours read nothing; LiteParse with Tesseract recovered 91% of the words and 34 of
  37 amounts, about a second a page (3 s on an Intel Mac mini).
- **OCRia-nano**, LiteParse's own OCR and table model, was better still on the scan, but its licence restricts use by
  larger companies and by anything that parses documents, and it has no Intel macOS build. Pulling it also changes
  how LiteParse reads tables, unless Tesseract is named.

The trial's verdict was LiteParse for scanned pages only. It was written on documents ours had been tuned on.

## The second trial: seven public documents neither had seen

Thirteen tables on seven public PDFs: an NJ Transit timetable, Tesla's Q3 2024 update (its statements of operations
and balance sheet), a Texas Instruments datasheet (SN74HC00), the Federal Reserve's H.8 release, the FOMC's September
2024 projections, a USGS open-file report (2016-1083) and an FDA nutrition facts FAQ. The truth for each table was
written from page images before either reader's output was read, and each sampled cell was scored by whether it sat
with its row's label and its column's heading.

| | Cells right | Rows right |
|---|---|---|
| Ours | 143/326 (44%) | 43/104 (41%) |
| LiteParse | 290/326 (89%) | 80/104 (77%) |

- **Ours fails** on financial statements, dense numeric tables, timetables and headings over several columns: it
  says the row labels as one list and each column as another. Its weak-table notes flagged most of those pages.
- **LiteParse fails** on footnote marks, fusing a mark into its figure ("20.1" for 0.1 with mark 2, "1275" for 275
  with mark 1), and drops empty cells in some ruled grids, which moves the figures after them into the wrong
  columns. For a knowledge base whose passages are said word for word, a wrong figure is the worst error.

- The H.8 table was found by neither (LiteParse reads it as text), and LiteParse's reading of the FOMC table runs its
  rows together; both are noted to the author as weak or as read differently by the two readers.
- The datasheet's electrical grid is read badly by both: neither is to be taken on trust there.
- Ours reads the FDA marks apart ("1 78"), which the strict scorer counts wrong; LiteParse's, once its marks are
  dropped, reads them all. Both still lose the mark in "(1)" (the USGS report's footnote references in a cell).
- The truth was written by a model from page images. A sample of it is worth checking by a person before these
  figures are quoted.

## What was decided (2026-11-27)

- **LiteParse reads a PDF by default** (`kb:ingest --reader liteparse`), Tesseract always named for OCR, the version
  pinned. Its text items go through kb-author's own page and heading logic, so sections, ids and pages come out as
  before; its own headings are not read.
- **Ours stays, as a second opinion and by choice.** On every page our table readers and checker read the same
  lines; where both found a table and a figure one reads is not in the other's, or the checker scores LiteParse's
  table weak, `kb:ingest` notes it for the author to check against the page. Neither reading is chosen silently.
  `--reader ours` reads as before, byte for byte.
- **A footnote mark LiteParse fuses into a figure is dropped**, by its size and raise, as ours drops a footnote
  marker.
- **A page with no text layer that LiteParse calls a scan is read by OCR**, each of its sections marked `ocr: true` in
  its source file and shown by `kb:status` and the review page, so the person approving reads it against the page. A
  page with no text layer that is a drawing (the datasheet's package outlines) is noted and not read.
- **A table only ours finds**, where LiteParse reads text, is noted as well.
- **The provenance records the reader**, and `kb:refresh` reads a source with the reader it names (none: ours), so a
  knowledge base ingested before this reads as it did; `kb:refresh --reader liteparse` moves it on purpose, and the
  passages whose sections then read differently wait for a person's approval.
- OCRia-nano is not used, for its licence and its missing Intel macOS build.
