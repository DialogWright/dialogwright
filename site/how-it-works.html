How it works

Callers talk naturally, and the system maps what they mean to the right action. Three parts, each with one job: the model never decides, and the code never guesses.

The three parts

Replies are fast because a decision is a short typed question, not a composed answer. How the model is asked is on a page of its own: decision models.

  1. 1 The model understands

    A decision model reads what the caller said and answers typed questions: is this a request to reschedule, which of these three appointments, what date was given. It returns judgments with probabilities, never generated text. Callers can say it in their own words, give three answers at once or change their mind.

  2. 2 Code decides

    Deterministic code owns the dialog: which form is active, which slot to ask next, when to read back, when to hand off to a person. The same judgments and state always lead to the same step, and every line the caller hears is an approved line filled with checked values, unless the app has asked for a generated one (below).

  3. 3 The gate checks every action

    Before any tool runs, a policy gate checks the caller's identity level, whose record it is, exact confirmation, role and attempt limits. The gate does not read the conversation, so no one can talk past it. Every decision, allowed or refused, goes to a hash-chained audit log.

  • Reproducible. Replay a call against its recorded model answers and get the same outcome.
  • Explainable. The audit names the rule that passed or failed, and what it compared.
  • Not steerable by the caller. What a caller says is data the model classifies, never instructions the code follows.
  • Tunable. Thresholds are numbers, adjusted by measurement rather than by rewording a prompt.

An app is a folder

YAML for what is data: intents, forms, slots, prompts, policy, identity, locales. TypeScript for what runs: tools, form hooks, rules of your own. defineApp joins the two. Most slots are configuration: name a type from the slot library (digits, choice, date, birthdate, name, record, text) and give it a few options.

The authoring guide takes the folder a file at a time: Authoring an app.

pnpm check says what to fix

It reads every YAML file against its schema and cross-checks the folder against the code: a slot a form names that does not exist, a line the engine needs that no prompt provides, a tool with no policy. Each problem is one line, file:line:column  path  message  ->  fix, written for a person or an assistant to act on. Here is the line it prints for a misspelt slot name in the utility's forms.yaml, and the run after the fix: 

$ pnpm check
forms.yaml:11:20  forms.report_outage.slots[1]  slot "symptm" is not defined  ->  rename it to "symptom", or add "symptm:" to slots.yaml (a library type, or { type: code } with the slot in src/app.ts (code.slots.symptm))

$ pnpm check
apps/clinic: ok
apps/harbor: ok
apps/secretary: ok
apps/utility: ok

Tested before a call is taken

  • Labelled utterances and scripted calls. Each app keeps what callers say, labelled, and whole calls with the outcome each must reach. The regression runs them against a committed baseline, and a changed outcome is a finding to explain.
  • Recorded calls. The real model's answers are recorded once into a cassette, so the same calls replay offline, with no key, to the same outcome.
  • The policy matrix. For every action and every kind of caller, what the gate decides, compared by a test (below).
  • The adversarial suite. Cases generated from the app itself: injections, relationships claimed and not proven, off-scope requests, codes said aloud, hostile replies from every tool. Each is held to properties over its run, never to lines, and a failing case is fixed in the app or the engine.

Built for AI coding assistants

The repository is written to be read by an AI coding assistant as well as by you. A root CLAUDE.md gives the roles, the commands and the rules, llms.txt indexes the docs, and every app has a CLAUDE.md of its own.

The create-app skill

The procedure an assistant follows to turn a paragraph into an app: a worksheet, the mapping onto slot types, policy and identity, the scaffold, the corpus and scripted calls, then the checks until they pass. It comes with a page of verified patterns and a corpus guide.

Read the skill

pnpm create-app <name>

The scaffold the skill calls. It writes a small app under apps/<name> that passes pnpm check, its own tests and its regression as created. Add --identity for an app that verifies its callers.

Where to start

We tried it

Two fresh assistants, with no context from the people who built the framework, were each given one paragraph (a fictional electric utility, a fictional transit agency) and told to use the skill. Each built a working app, in about 15 and 11 minutes by their own count, with pnpm check and the scripted calls green. Both caught the same real hole in their own policy by reading the policy matrix: a delegate could have had a one-time code texted to someone else's phone. The engine now refuses that for every app. Every stumble they logged was fixed or recorded with its reason.

Compliance can read it

Policy is a file, not code. policy.yaml names the rules each action runs, and identity.yaml says who the app serves and how a caller proves who they are. Both compile to what the gate runs. Three generated pages make a change readable by someone who does not write code:

  • POLICY.md, the policy card: each action and each rule in plain English, with diagrams, written from the same compiled policy the gate runs. It lists every line a model words and every prescribed line.
  • policy.matrix: for every action and every kind of caller, the verdict and its reason, so a policy change shows up as a diff of what the gate decides.
  • APP-MAP.md: the app's intents, forms, slots, actions and rules as diagrams.

Each is written by a command and compared by a test, never written by CI. CODEOWNERS makes a change to the policy files and these pages need compliance's review.

From one machine to many servers

From a personal line on one machine you own to a contact center across many servers, it is the same app: what it understands, what it says and what the policy allows do not change.

On a machine you own

Try it on a laptop, then run it on any machine that stays on, for a small office or your own line. A phone line needs two keys, a carrier's (Telnyx or Twilio) and a decision model's, and a tunnel (a quick one needs no account). It starts at boot, survives its own restarts without dropping a caller, and shows you calls on your phone.

Run it on a machine you own

Across many servers

Several servers behind a load balancer, with sessions, the audit and the traces in shared stores (Postgres and S3-compatible storage). When the server holding a call stops or crashes, another takes the call over from the shared session. Built for Phase 8b's scope; roles, single sign-on for the console and OpenTelemetry are left for later.

Scaling out

  • The same app folder. Intents, forms, prompts and slots in YAML, tools in TypeScript.
  • The same policy. policy.yaml and the gate decide every action, on one machine or many.
  • The same audit. Every decision, allowed or refused, in a hash-chained log, with a trace of every turn.
  • The same tests. pnpm check, the policy matrix and the scripted calls, before any call is taken.

Answers from a knowledge base

Callers ask questions a document answers: is this covered, how long do I have, are you open on Saturday. An app answers them from a knowledge base in its folder: topics, and for each a passage written from a section of a source document, which a person has read against that section and approved. Nothing is said that no one approved: a passage whose source changes, or that is edited after its approval, is withheld until a person approves it again.

  • Found on your own machine. Retrieval matches the caller's words to the topics by their keywords and by their meaning, with a small embedding model that runs locally, with no API call. Its settings are chosen offline, on paraphrases the topics have never seen: Harbor Mutual finds the right topic among the first eight for 95.8% of 120 of them. A larger model can be pinned for long documents; where its native runtime has no build for the machine, or a restarted server cannot reach the model's repository, the same files run on WebAssembly from the local cache.
  • The decision model chooses; it never writes. It is asked which of the topics found the caller asked about, whether they asked something none of them answers, or nothing yet. A question nothing answers is told so, in the app's own words, with what can be asked.
  • Three levels, chosen topic by topic. Prescribed: an answer is its approved passage, word for word. Worded, with a prescribed fallback: where the app marks the topic risk: low, the passage may be worded for what the caller asked, held to the passage by the same checks and judge as any generated line, and the passage is said whenever a check fails or the wording is late. Generated from approved documents: where no topic answers, a model writes the answer from the sections of documents a person approved for summarizing, and when a check fails the caller hears the document's own lines that answer the question, word for word, under their section's heading. Past a line's budget the caller hears a hold line first.
  • Grounded in the documents. For an answer from documents, the decision model reads each section retrieval offers and answers one yes-or-no question for each: would it answer the caller's question? A model words the answer from the chosen sections alone; code refuses any figure the sections do not contain, and the drift judge refuses an answer that adds, changes or drops anything that bears on the question. On Harbor Mutual's member handbook the right section is offered for 97.5% of 80 held-out questions, and the judge refused all 20 unfaithful answers in its labelled set and passed all 20 faithful ones.
  • Follow-up questions. "What about for a family?" after "what's my deductible?" is read with the earlier question, with no second model call: the sections are found for the follow-up's words alone and with the earlier question, the earlier answer's sections and those under them are offered too, and the decision model reads the caller's question first and the earlier one only where the new one cannot stand alone. On a 123-page benefit booklet, follow-ups answered from a right section went from 21 of 42 to 38 of 43, none found nothing (7 before), and questions that stand on their own gave the same answers as before. It is an option, measured per app with pnpm kb:bakeoff --follow-ups.
  • Answers can depend on who is asking. A passage applies by facts code reads from the system of record (the member's plan), never from what the caller said, and a gated action can add the caller's own data after it ("You've used 4 of them this year.").
  • What nothing answered is ranked. pnpm kb:gaps reads the calls' traces and ranks the questions the knowledge base did not answer, each with its fix: words for a topic, a new passage, a section of a source to draft one from. For an answer from documents it also lists the sections that were not offered on the call but rank for the question now, so a hint goes to the section that answers, not the one that came nearest.

On the web, too

The app that answers the phone answers a web chat with the same intents, forms, policy and knowledge base. A site embeds the widget, one script with no dependencies, or the engine serves the app a chat page of its own. A chat can show more than a call can say, so each of these is an option of the app's, and a call is unchanged by it.

  • A chat page of its own. app.yaml's brand (a logo, a tagline, colours) and chat.page: the conversation front and centre, ways to start, a number to call, and sections under it that say what the line does.
  • Formatted where a chat can show it. A line may give its words on a chat (lists, bold, italic) while a call says its text. Every value is filled in as plain text after the line is read, so nothing a caller or a system says can become formatting.
  • Answer buttons. With chat.replies, a question with a known set of answers (yes or no, a choice, one of the caller's records) shows them as buttons. A tap sends its words as typed words are sent: the buttons decide nothing.
  • Where an answer comes from. With chat.sources, an answer from the knowledge base shows the document, the section as the document prints it, and the page, under the answer.
  • A chat-only server. VOICE_PROVIDERS=none runs the web chat with no carrier, no phone number and no carrier's secret.

Generated wording, where you opt in

Every line is said word for word unless the app asks for a generated one, a line at a time (mode: generative) or a form at a time (wording: generative). What is regulated is the app designer's call: a line marked prescribed: true is never generated, and the policy card lists every generated and every prescribed line. The model chooses the words, never the content: the core has decided before any line is worded.

To summarize what a caller said, the app frames it: the line is fixed and says whose words follow, and only a span of it is generated. The front desk's read-back is the example:

  • Checked before anyone hears it. Code holds the span to its data and shape: every value word for word, no figure it was not given, its word budget, one sentence. What it means is the drift judge's: the decision model, asked whether the span, heard in its sentence, is a faithful summary, and a summary that names anyone or anything the caller did not is not. That is a judgment, not a guarantee, and it is measured (the judge).
  • Any failure says the exact words. A check that fails, a slow model or an error, and the caller hears their own message word for word, in the line's other frame.
  • Within a budget. A line's wording has 900 ms on a call and 1500 ms in a chat by default, and its checks 500 ms and 1000 ms; past either, the fixed text is said.
  • The model is yours to choose. The examples word their lines with Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite (a preview, through OpenRouter), named in the app's style.yaml. In the front desk's bake-off of four models on the same requests it wrote the summaries fastest, in 491 ms at the median and 841 ms at p95, and no summary turned who does what. A line is about 500 tokens in and 40 out: a month of the maintainer's recordings with it, every generated line of the front desk and Harbor Mutual recorded again and again, came to about 2 cents in all. pnpm render:bakeoff compares models on an app's own lines.
  • The message itself is never changed. The caller's yes covers what the slots hold, and the message is delivered as they said it.

Tuning with live calls

An app green on its recordings has been tried on its own words. A live call adds a carrier, a recognizer, a room and a person who does not know the script. Chapter 17 of the guide is the working method that follows, with examples from a day of live calls to the front desk:

  • trace every call, and read it turn by turn in the console, each question beside its answer;
  • tune on a private copy that keeps the caller's words, when the shipped app keeps none;
  • treat a score near 0.5 as a coin toss, and suspect the question's words before the threshold;
  • change the words, record again, and triage the recording;
  • choose the render model and the judge by measurement, against the call's time budgets.
